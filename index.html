What exists India already built the hard parts

Every piece is here

India didn't fall behind on technology. It built payment systems the rest of the world studies. The commute is where those pieces stop joining up.

UPI

Pay anyone in seconds

From street stalls to rent, phone payments are everyday life across India.

FASTag

Cars pay tolls without stopping

A tag on the windscreen pays as the car rolls through. A commuter on foot has nothing like it across modes.

1.04 crore

Tickets sold in Mumbai One

MMRDA's app links 11 operators and sold this many tickets in about ten months. But about 80% were Metro tickets, and it can't sell local train season passes.

NCMC

One national card standard

The National Common Mobility Card works at Metro Lines 1, 2A, 3 and 7 and on BEST buses. No source we found shows it working on local trains.

Local trains carry the most riders, and they sit outside: tickets and passes moved to RailOne when the UTS app shut on 1 March 2026.

Sydney What it looks like when it works

Tap on. Tap off. Travel.

In Sydney, one Opal card works on trains, buses, ferries, Metro and light rail. You never buy a ticket. The system works out the fare and stops charging once you hit the daily or weekly cap.

To be precise

Tap payment already exists in parts of Mumbai: Metro gates accept NCMC cards, and BEST conductors can accept them too. What's missing is one card that works on everything, especially local trains, with one fare and one weekly limit across the whole trip.

Transfer discounts matter most

Two-thirds of this commuter's weekly spend is Metro fares. Rewarding the switch between modes helps Mumbai more than caps alone.

Trains need their own cap

Pay-per-tap would cost rail commuters about three times their pass. Capping rail at the season pass price keeps them no worse off.

Caps mostly help long Metro trips

A full-length Metro 3 rider would pay ₹350 instead of ₹700 a week. That's where most of the subsidy would go.

Making it real Who runs it and what it costs

One tap, many operators

The commuter sees one fare. Behind it, a clearing house splits the money between operators by distance travelled, and the state pays for the discounts.

Commuter tapsOne NCMC card or phone, on every leg
OneTap accountRun by MMRDA as the integrator
Clearing houseSplits each fare by distance per operator
Operators paidIndian Railways, Metro lines, BEST, TMT, NMMT and more

This has been tried before: an earlier ₹140 crore single-ticket plan for Mumbai stalled when the state and Railways couldn't agree on payments. A formal agreement with Indian Railways comes first.

What it costs to build

On the OneTap bar, solid yellow is our low estimate and the lighter part shows how high it could go. Mumbai costs far less because Metro gates and BEST buses already read NCMC cards. The main new build is tap readers at about 119 local train stations, plus the account and fare system. Order-of-magnitude estimates, not vendor quotes.

₹52 crore

The real decision is the discount, not the technology. Every 1 lakh commuters who save ₹100 a week costs about ₹52 crore a year. If 5 lakh benefit, that's ₹260 crore a year, more than the whole build within two years. Fewer paper tickets and less fare evasion would offset part of it.

Roadmap Four phases, three checkpoints

Start small, prove it, then grow

  1. Months 0–6

    Prove it

    Measure how many commuters combine modes, and price readers for stations.

    Checkpoint: operators sign on
  2. Months 6–12

    Link accounts

    One login across Mumbai One and RailOne, and one balance.

    Checkpoint: Railways agreement
  3. Year 2

    Pilot fares

    Western line locals, Metro 1 and BEST feeder buses, with the transfer discount.

    Checkpoint: pilot meets targets
  4. Year 3 onwards

    Scale up

    All 11 operators, daily and weekly caps, and a template for other Indian cities.

Risks What could go wrong

The hard parts are not technical

Railways may not join

Local trains are run by Indian Railways, not the state. Response: pilot on one line first, and agree revenue sharing in writing before anything else.

Who pays for the discounts

Caps and transfer discounts cost money every year. Response: fund them as a planned state subsidy, and tune the cap in the pilot.

Crowds at readers

Some stations handle huge peak crowds, and gates would block them. Response: stand-mounted readers, as at Sydney's ungated stations.

Forgetting to tap off

Without tap-off, distance fares can't be worked out. Response: charge a default fare, as Opal does, and send reminders.

People without cards or phones

Many commuters still pay cash. Response: keep paper tickets during the transition, and sell cards at counters.

Card fees

BEST charges 1% plus GST on NCMC payments today. Response: agree one fee structure across all operators.

Why I made this

When I started riding public transport in Sydney every day, I noticed how little I had to think about paying. One card, one tap, on any train, bus or ferry.

It made me think of India. India moved hundreds of millions of people from cash to phone payments in just a few years, something few countries have managed. Yet getting around a city still means juggling apps, tickets and cash. For daily commuters, and for the tourists who visit every year, that should be the easy part.

OneTap Bharat is my idea for closing that gap. I believe it can be built, and that it could be one of the best things India does for its cities.
